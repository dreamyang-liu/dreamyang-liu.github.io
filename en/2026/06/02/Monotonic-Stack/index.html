Monotonic Stack

2026-06-02
monotonic-stack stack array

Keep only the candidates still waiting for their next greater element. When a new value arrives, pop every stack top that is — each one’s “next greater” is exactly . Every element is pushed once and popped once: total.

Intuition — “pop stack tops that can no longer be the answer”

Thinking forward — “for each , find the first element to the right that’s larger” — is . Flip the perspective: instead, each new element actively claims whose answer it is.

The stack maintains a list of indices of candidates that haven’t yet found their next greater element, strictly decreasing from bottom to top (earlier arrivals are larger). When arrives:

  • Stack top ? Then is the stack top’s “next greater”. Pop it and record the answer.
  • Keep comparing the next stack top, until the top is or the stack is empty.
  • Push onto the stack and wait for its own “next greater”.

Each index is pushed once and popped at most once — 2n total operations, amortized .

In one line: the stack and the target are opposite. To find something larger, the stack holds candidates sorted large-to-small.

Animation — watch the color of the stack top

The animation below runs through array [2, 1, 5, 6, 2, 3] finding “next greater element”. Step through it one click at a time, keeping an eye on three things:

  1. Green is the current nums[i] being visited.
  2. Cyan are the candidates still in the stack, waiting for their answer.
  3. Red is the stack top being popped by x — the →x floating above it is the recorded answer.

Pay special attention when 5 arrives and pops both 1 and 2 in one go, and when 6 pops 5 too — that’s the true face of “ amortized”: paying off multiple old debts at once.

After watching the animation, the stack’s shape is always a decreasing staircase of cyan columns. That picture is the core mental image for monotonic stacks.

One template, four variants

There’s just one template; change two things to get any variant. The stack’s monotonicity is the opposite of the target — that’s the core.

Target Stack monotonicity (bottom→top) while condition Traversal direction
Next greater Decreasing nums[top] <= x Left to right
Next smaller Increasing nums[top] >= x Left to right
Previous greater Decreasing nums[top] <= x Right to left
Previous smaller Increasing nums[top] >= x Right to left

Mnemonic: “Greater uses decreasing; smaller uses increasing. Looking left means traverse right, looking right means traverse left.”

As for whether to include the equality in the comparison, it depends on whether equal elements can serve as each other’s answer — including it is usually the safer default.

Advanced — Largest Rectangle in Histogram (LC 84)

Idea: enumerate “use this bar as the height” for the maximum rectangle. To determine the width, you need to know the first shorter bar on the left and right. That’s exactly the “previous smaller + next smaller” two variants — handled in a single pass with the same stack.

What the two sentinel 0s do:

  • The trailing 0 forces all real bars to be flushed from the stack, handled uniformly inside the loop.
  • The leading 0 acts as the left boundary, ensuring stack[-1] never goes out of bounds on a pop.

Memory aid

One sentence: “When the stack top is beaten by , is the stack top’s answer.”

Three steps:

  1. Recognize the problem type: “next/previous greater/smaller”, “first bar to the right shorter/taller than …”, “histogram / rain water” → reflexively write a monotonic stack.
  2. Pick the direction: looking for “greater” → decreasing stack; looking for “smaller” → increasing stack. Stack and target are opposites.
  3. Pop and settle immediately: inside the while loop when you pop the top, the current i and the new top are its right and left boundaries respectively — distance, width, and contribution are all computed in that instant.

Quick decision guide:

  • Single point + “next X” → monotonic stack
  • Sliding window + “interval extremum” → monotonic deque (add an expiry check)
  • Offline + “comparison queries” → offline scan + monotonic stack
